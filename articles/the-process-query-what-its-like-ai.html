The Process Query: How AI Answers 'What's It Like to Work With You?'

Scott TischlerFounder & Chairman, AIrecommend.ai · November 20, 2026 · 9 min read
⤓ Download PDFVisit AIrecommend.ai

There's a question buyers ask now that almost no business writes for: what is it actually like to work with you? Not what you sell, not what it costs — what the experience is. What are the steps, how long does it take, what's expected of me, where does it usually go wrong. People used to answer that by calling three references. Increasingly they ask an answer engine instead. And if you've never published a clear account of your own process, the model doesn't skip the question. It answers anyway, from reviews, from third parties, or from the generic pattern of your category — which means a stranger's data, not yours, becomes the description of what you're like to hire.

This is one of the most overlooked queries in AI visibility, because it doesn't look like a visibility query at all. It's not "best provider in my area." It's softer, more human, and far more decisive: someone who is close to buying wants to know what they're walking into. My read is that these experiential questions are quietly becoming some of the highest-stakes moments in the buying journey, precisely because they happen late, when the person is nearly ready to commit and is looking for a reason to feel safe or a reason to back out. What AI says in that moment matters enormously, and most businesses have handed the answer to chance.

The query you're not writing for

Walk through how your own content is built and you'll notice a pattern. You've probably written about what you do, what you offer, maybe why you're different. That content answers the early-stage questions — the ones people ask when they're figuring out whether they need you at all. What's usually missing is anything that answers the late-stage, experiential question: what happens after I say yes.

Those questions take a lot of shapes, and they rarely use the word "process." What's it like working with you. What should I expect. How does this actually go. What's the onboarding like. How long until I see results. What do you need from me. What happens if something goes wrong. Will I be dealing with you or getting handed off. These are the questions a cautious buyer asks a friend over coffee, and they're exactly the questions people now type into an answer engine because it feels lower-stakes than asking you directly. The person asking is not shopping. They're close. And the content that would satisfy them — a plain, honest account of how working with you unfolds — is the content most businesses have simply never created.

That absence is the whole problem. You can be excellent at the actual experience and invisible on the question about it, because being good at something and having written about it are two entirely different things, and the model can only use the second one.

Where AI gets its answer when you're silent

Here's the part worth sitting with: the model will answer the experiential question whether or not you've given it material. When you've said nothing, it reaches for whatever is available, and the sources it falls back on are ones you control far less than your own site.

The first fallback is reviews. If customers describe the experience of working with you — in their words, with their emphasis, including their complaints — that's what the model has to work with, and it will characterize your process from that. Reviews are valuable and you want them, but a review-sourced description of your process is shaped by whoever happened to write, which is often the delighted and the furious and rarely the representative middle. The second fallback is third parties: directories, forums, comparison sites, anywhere people discuss what you're like to deal with. The third, and the one that should bother you most, is inference from your category. When the model has nothing specific about you, it can describe the typical experience of businesses like yours — the generic pattern — and attach it to your name. That means the slowest, most frustrating version of your industry's reputation can become the default answer about you, simply because you never published anything to override it.

Notice what all three fallbacks have in common: none of them is your account of your own process. You've effectively let the market, the complainers, and the category average narrate your experience for you. That's not a failure of the model. It's doing the reasonable thing with the only material it has. The failure is upstream — you never wrote the thing that would have given it something better.

How to write the process so AI can narrate it

The fix is to create the content that answers the experiential question directly, in a form a model can read and repeat. This is not a sales page and it shouldn't read like one. The buyer at this stage can smell spin, and so, increasingly, can the systems summarizing you. What works is a plain, specific, honest walk-through of what working with you is actually like. A few things make that content do its job.

  • Lay out the actual steps, in order. Name the stages from first contact to ongoing work — what happens first, what happens next, what the hand-offs are. A clear sequence is something a model can lift and narrate as "here's how it works," which is precisely the answer the query wants. Vague reassurance that "we make it easy" gives the model nothing to repeat.
  • Set expectations with real specifics. How long does a typical engagement or onboarding take? When do people usually start seeing results? What does the first week look like? Specific, honest answers — even ranges — are far more quotable and more trusted than smooth generalities. If it varies, say what it varies with. That's more credible, not less.
  • Say what you need from the customer. The experiential question is partly "what will be expected of me." Spelling out the customer's role — what they'll need to provide, decide, or show up for — answers a real anxiety and signals that you've done this enough to know how it goes.
  • Be honest about friction. Where does it usually get hard? What surprises people? What's not a fit? A business willing to name the rough patches reads as credible to a human and gives the model balanced material instead of pure marketing — which matters, because answer engines increasingly weigh sources that acknowledge limitations over ones that only sell. Honest hedging is a feature here, not a weakness.
  • Write it in plain language, as answers to real questions. Frame sections the way people actually ask — "how long does it take," "what happens after you sign on" — so your content matches the shape of the query. The closer your phrasing is to the question in someone's head, the more directly the model can map one to the other.

Put together, that's a process or "what to expect" account that does something your sales copy never could: it gives the answer engine your own, specific, credible version of the experience, so that when someone asks what you're like to work with, the model has something better than a review average or a category stereotype to reach for. You're not manipulating the answer. You're finally supplying it.

What this does and doesn't fix

I want to be clear about the limits. Writing a great process page does not override a genuinely bad experience. If customers consistently describe working with you as painful, the honest fix is the experience itself, not the content about it — and reviews will keep telling the real story no matter what you publish. This strategy is for businesses whose actual experience is good or at least solid, and who have simply never translated that into anything the model can see. For them, it closes a real and costly gap.

It also doesn't mean reviews stop mattering — they still carry heavy weight, and often more than your own page, because they're corroboration and you're assertion. The point isn't to replace what customers say; it's to stop being silent next to it. When your own clear account of your process sits alongside consistent reviews that describe the same experience, the two reinforce each other, and the model has a coherent, well-sourced picture to summarize. When you're silent, it has only one side, shaped by whoever chose to write it.

The practical move is to ask what your best prospects want to know right before they commit, and notice how little of your content actually answers it. Then write the honest walk-through: the steps in order, the real timelines, what you need from them, where it gets hard. Publish it in plain language, phrased the way people ask. It's unglamorous content that no one brags about producing, and it quietly determines what an answer engine tells a nearly-decided buyer about what you're like to hire. Right now, for most businesses, something other than you is answering that question. This is how you take it back.

Key takeaways

  • Late-stage buyers ask AI an experiential question — 'what's it actually like to work with you?' — and most businesses have published nothing that answers it.
  • The model answers anyway. When you're silent it falls back on reviews, third-party chatter, or a generic category stereotype — none of which is your account of your own process.
  • The riskiest fallback is category inference: with nothing specific about you, AI can attach the typical (often slowest, most frustrating) version of your industry's reputation to your name.
  • Write a plain, honest 'what to expect' walk-through: the steps in order, real timelines, what you need from the customer, and where it usually gets hard — framed as the questions people actually ask.
  • Honesty about friction is an asset here — answer engines increasingly favor sources that acknowledge limitations over pure marketing, and cautious buyers trust it more too.
  • This won't fix a genuinely bad experience (reviews will keep telling the real story), and it doesn't replace reviews — it stops you being silent beside them so the two reinforce each other.

Frequently asked questions

Isn't 'what's it like to work with you' just answered by my reviews?
Reviews are part of it, but they're an incomplete and skewed source for this question. They're written by whoever felt moved to write — often the delighted and the furious — so a review-only picture of your process reflects the extremes, not the typical experience. And if your reviews are thin, the model falls back on third-party chatter or the generic pattern of your category. Publishing your own clear, honest account of the process gives AI something specific from you to sit alongside the reviews, so the answer isn't shaped entirely by other people's data.
What should a 'process' or 'what to expect' page actually contain?
Lay out the real steps in order, from first contact through ongoing work, with the hand-offs named. Set honest expectations with specifics — how long onboarding takes, when people typically see results, what the first week looks like — using ranges if it varies. Say what you need from the customer. And be honest about where it gets hard or who it's not a fit for. Write it in plain language, framed as the questions people actually ask, so the content matches the shape of the query. The goal is a credible walk-through, not a sales pitch.
If my actual process is messy, should I still write this?
Be careful. Publishing a polished process page won't override a genuinely bad experience — reviews and word of mouth will keep telling the real story, and overselling only widens the gap between what you claim and what people report, which answer engines can pick up on. If the experience itself is the problem, fix that first. This strategy is for businesses whose process is genuinely good or solid but who've never described it in a form AI can read. For them, writing the honest walk-through closes a real gap; for a broken process, it just documents the breakage.
