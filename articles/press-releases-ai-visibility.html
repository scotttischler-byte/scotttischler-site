Do Press Releases Still Work for AI Visibility? An Honest Answer

Scott TischlerFounder & Chairman, AIrecommend.ai · November 22, 2026 · 8 min read
⤓ Download PDFVisit AIrecommend.ai

Mostly no, with a narrow and important exception. The old-model press release — pay a wire service, blast a corporate announcement to hundreds of sites, watch it get republished verbatim on low-quality news aggregators — does almost nothing for AI visibility, and can occasionally hurt you. But the thing a press release is supposed to produce, which is genuine third-party coverage from sources AI actually trusts, remains one of the most valuable assets you can build. The distinction between those two things is the whole answer.

I get asked about press releases constantly, usually in the form "should we put out a press release to get picked up by ChatGPT?" It's a reasonable instinct — press releases were a real SEO tactic for years, and people assume the AI era rewards the same moves. It mostly doesn't. So let me walk through why the mechanical press release falls flat, where it can still earn its keep, and what to do instead if your real goal is getting AI to cite and recommend you.

Why the mechanical press release does little for AI

Start with how AI decides who to trust. When a model builds an answer about a business, it's weighing corroboration — does credible, independent information agree on who you are and what you're known for? The strongest signal is third-party validation: real people and real publications, writing about you on their own initiative, in places with established authority. A press release fails this test on almost every axis, and it's worth being specific about why.

First, it's self-authored. A press release is you talking about you, dressed up in the third person. Models are increasingly good at recognizing promotional, first-party content for what it is, and self-description carries far less weight than independent corroboration. Saying "Company X is a leading provider" in your own release doesn't make the model believe it; it's exactly the kind of claim that needs outside backing to count.

Second, the distribution produces duplication, not authority. The classic wire release gets republished verbatim across a network of syndication sites. That creates a lot of identical copies of the same text on low-trust domains — and duplication across thin sources is close to the opposite of corroboration. Ten sites running the same paragraph you wrote aren't ten independent confirmations; to a model they read as one piece of promotional content echoed ten times. It can even muddy your entity signals, scattering slightly-off versions of your description across the web.

Third, the venues are usually low-authority. The sites that automatically republish wire releases tend not to be the sources AI leans on when it wants reliable information. Getting "coverage" on a site no one — human or model — treats as authoritative isn't coverage in any meaningful sense. My honest read is that the typical paid-wire press release is a cost with almost no AI-visibility return, and occasionally a small negative if it pollutes how you're described.

The narrow case where a release still earns its keep

Here's the exception, and it matters. A press release is a format, and the format isn't the problem — the mechanical blast-and-syndicate use of it is. There are situations where issuing a clear, factual announcement is genuinely useful.

The first is when you have real, newsworthy news and you're using the release as a source document for journalists who might actually cover it. A funding round, a genuine first, a notable hire, a real milestone — handed to reporters who could turn it into independent coverage — can seed the thing that does matter: a journalist writing about you in a publication AI trusts. In that use, the release isn't the asset; it's the raw material that helps someone credible create the asset.

The second is as a primary-source record of a verifiable fact. If something about your business genuinely changed — a new location, a certification, an acquisition — having a clear, dated, factual statement of it on your own site and in a few legitimate places helps the record stay accurate. That's less about chasing a citation and more about not letting AI carry stale or wrong facts about you. It's a defensive, housekeeping use, and it's fine.

Notice what both exceptions have in common: the value comes from truth and from enabling real third parties, not from distribution volume. Neither one is "pay for syndication and hope the robots notice." If you catch yourself buying a release for the number of sites it lands on, you're back in the tactic that doesn't work.

What to do instead if the goal is AI citations

If what you actually want is to be cited and recommended by AI, redirect the budget and effort you'd spend on a press release toward building real corroboration. That's harder and slower, which is exactly why it's worth more — anyone can buy a wire release, which is part of why it counts for so little.

Pursue genuine earned media. A single article about you in a publication that AI treats as authoritative is worth more than a hundred syndicated copies of your own announcement. That means pitching real stories to real journalists, offering a useful perspective rather than a self-congratulatory announcement, and being the kind of source a writer wants to quote. It's relationship and substance work, not distribution work.

Get yourself included in third-party lists and roundups — the "best X for Y" articles, the industry comparisons, the curated recommendations. Models lean heavily on these when building their own best-of answers, and being named in them by someone other than you is high-grade corroboration. Contribute expertise where it gets attributed to you: guest articles, podcast appearances, expert commentary. These put your name and point of view into trusted venues in a way that reads as authority rather than advertising.

And make sure the basics of your own entity are clean and consistent, so that when real coverage does appear, it reinforces a coherent picture rather than a scattered one. The point is cumulative: independent sources, saying consistent things about you, in places that carry weight. That's what moves an AI from "never heard of them" to "here's who you should consider." A press release is a shortcut that skips the only part that matters.

A word on the new "AI-optimized press release" pitch

A fresh version of this is making the rounds: services that promise an "AI-optimized" or "GEO press release" engineered to get you cited by ChatGPT and friends. I'd treat these with healthy skepticism. Dressing a press release in schema markup, question-and-answer formatting, and extractable phrasing can make the text more machine-readable, and that's not nothing — but it doesn't fix the two things that actually hold a press release back. It's still self-authored, and it's still distributed the same low-authority way. You can make a promotional document beautifully structured and it will still be a promotional document the model discounts.

The deeper issue is that these pitches sell the format as the lever when the lever is corroboration. No amount of formatting converts "we said this about ourselves" into "an independent, trusted source confirmed this about them." If a service is promising AI citations as the deliverable of a press release, ask them how the release produces independent third-party validation rather than more copies of your own words. Usually it doesn't, and the honest ones will admit the release is really there to help you land genuine coverage — which puts you right back at the only thing that works.

None of this means formatting and structured content are a waste. On your own site, making content extractable and well-structured genuinely helps AI use it. The mistake is thinking that technique, applied to a self-promotional release on syndication sites, buys you the authority that only independent sources can confer. Good structure amplifies trustworthy content; it doesn't manufacture trust that isn't there.

The honest bottom line

If someone's selling you a press-release package on the promise that it'll get you into ChatGPT's answers, be skeptical — that's the version that mostly doesn't work. The format can still play a legitimate supporting role when you have real news to hand to real reporters, or a factual record to keep straight. But the engine of AI visibility is third-party corroboration from trusted sources, and there's no paid-distribution shortcut to it. Spend the effort where it compounds: earning coverage, getting listed, contributing expertise, and keeping your entity consistent. That's slower than hitting "send" on a wire release, and it's the difference between buying the appearance of authority and building the real thing the models can actually see.

Key takeaways

  • The classic blast-and-syndicate press release does little for AI visibility and can occasionally hurt by scattering self-authored, duplicated descriptions of you.
  • AI trusts third-party corroboration; a press release fails because it's self-authored, duplicated across low-trust sites, and published in venues models don't lean on.
  • The format still earns its keep in two narrow cases: as a source document handed to real journalists, and as a dated factual record that keeps the public record about you accurate.
  • The value in those exceptions comes from truth and from enabling real third parties — never from distribution volume or the number of sites it lands on.
  • If the goal is AI citations, redirect the budget to earned media, third-party lists, attributed expertise, and a clean, consistent entity.
  • There's no paid-distribution shortcut to AI authority — independent sources saying consistent things about you in trusted places is what moves the model.

Frequently asked questions

Will a press release get my business into ChatGPT or Perplexity answers?
Generally no, if you mean the traditional paid-wire release that gets syndicated across many sites. AI weighs independent, third-party corroboration, and a press release is self-authored content duplicated across low-authority venues — close to the opposite of what earns trust. It can even muddy how you're described. The exception is when the release is a source document that leads to genuine coverage from a journalist in a publication AI actually trusts.
Is there ever a good reason to issue a press release anymore?
Two, and both are about substance rather than distribution. First, when you have real, newsworthy news and you hand the release to reporters who might write their own independent coverage — there the release is raw material, not the asset. Second, as a clear, dated factual record of a real change (a new location, a certification, an acquisition) that helps keep the public record about you accurate so AI doesn't carry stale facts. Neither is 'pay for syndication and hope.'
What should I do instead to get cited and recommended by AI?
Build real corroboration: pitch genuine stories to journalists for earned media, get included in third-party 'best of' lists and roundups, contribute attributed expertise through guest articles and podcasts, and keep your own entity signals clean and consistent. A single article about you in a source AI trusts is worth more than a hundred syndicated copies of your own announcement, because it's independent — and independence is the whole point.
