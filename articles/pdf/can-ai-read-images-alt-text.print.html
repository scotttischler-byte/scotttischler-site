Can AI Read Your Images? Alt Text, Visual Content, and the Invisible Half of Your Website

By Scott Tischler · November 26, 2026 · 8 min read

Mostly no — today's AI search and recommendation systems read the text of your website far more reliably than they "see" your images. When a model decides who to recommend, it is overwhelmingly working from words it can crawl and quote, not from pictures it has to visually interpret. So if your menu is a photo, your prices live inside a graphic, your specs are baked into an infographic, or your key message is text-on-an-image, there's a good chance the systems deciding who gets named simply can't read any of it. This is my read as a practitioner, not a statement about what any specific model is technically capable of — vision models exist and are improving — but in terms of what actually drives recommendations today, the safe assumption is that your images are close to invisible, and alt text and captions are how you translate them back into something AI can use.

This catches a lot of good businesses off guard, because a page can look rich and informative to a human and be nearly empty to a machine. You see a beautiful menu, a clear price list, a detailed spec sheet. The model, reading the page's actual text, sees a headline, a couple of sentences, and a row of image files it can't open. The gap between how your page looks and what it says is exactly where AI visibility quietly leaks away.

What "reading an image" actually means for AI right now

It helps to separate two different things that get blurred together. One is whether an AI model can, in a lab sense, look at an image and describe it — many can. The other is whether the systems that build AI recommendations are actually doing that for your website at the moment they decide who to cite. Those are not the same question, and for practical purposes the second one is what matters.

When an AI answer grounds itself in real sources — the citations and recommendations businesses care about — it's typically pulling and reading the text of web pages. That pipeline is built around words: crawling them, matching them to a question, and quoting them back. An image on the page is, to that pipeline, mostly a file reference with some surrounding markup. If the only place a fact exists is inside the pixels of that image, the fact may never enter the model's working picture of you at all.

My rule of thumb is simple and deliberately conservative: assume any information that exists only as an image does not exist as far as AI recommendations are concerned. You might occasionally be pleasantly surprised. You should never plan around the surprise.

The information you're accidentally hiding in pixels

Once you adopt that assumption, you start noticing how much critical information businesses routinely lock inside images — the exact information a buyer would ask an AI about.

  • Menus and price lists as photos. Restaurants and service businesses love to upload a nicely designed menu as a single image. To a human it's perfect. To a model asked "what does this place serve and what does it cost," it's a blank. Every dish, every price, every description — unreadable.
  • Product specs and comparison tables. When the specs that make your product the right choice live inside a graphic, the model can't match them to the detailed, specific questions buyers ask. You lose exactly the queries where your details would have won.
  • Infographics and "everything in one image." The irony of the well-made infographic is that it concentrates your best, most quotable information into the one format AI is least able to read. The clearer and more self-contained the graphic, the more you've hidden.
  • Hours, contact details and key claims baked into banners. Design-driven sites sometimes render hours, location or headline claims as part of an image. If it's not also in the text, it's not reliably available to the model when it answers a time-sensitive or location-sensitive question.

The common thread is that the more visually polished and self-contained an element is, the more likely its information exists only as pixels — and the more completely it's hidden from the systems that recommend you.

Alt text, captions, and surrounding text — the three translations

The fix isn't to strip the visuals out. Images do real work for humans, and humans still have to choose you after the AI names you. The fix is to make sure every image's information also exists in a form the model can read. There are three layers of translation, and they stack.

Alt text is the most direct. It's the text alternative attached to an image, and it's genuinely read by machines. The mistake most businesses make is treating it as a throwaway label — "menu.jpg," "photo of our store." Useful alt text describes the actual information: not "our menu" but a real description of what the menu contains. It's the difference between telling the model a picture exists and telling it what the picture says.

Captions and nearby text are the second layer, and often the more powerful one, because body text is the thing AI pipelines read most reliably. A caption under your infographic that states its key points in words, or a paragraph near your price image that lists the prices in plain text, puts the information squarely in the channel the model trusts. You're not describing the image; you're restating its content where the model will actually find it.

The real text version is the third and strongest layer: wherever the information genuinely matters — your menu, your prices, your specs, your hours — have a true text version on the page, not just a picture of one. Keep the beautiful image for humans by all means. But let the text carry the facts, because the text is what gets read, matched and quoted.

The one exception worth knowing

I want to be fair to the other side of this, because the picture isn't completely one-sided. There are situations where an image's information does find its way to AI, and knowing them keeps you from over-correcting.

The most common is when the information in your image also exists somewhere else the model can read — your own text elsewhere on the site, a third-party listing, a review that quotes your prices, a directory that carries your hours. In those cases the model may answer correctly about your image's content, but notice why: it's not reading your picture, it's reading someone's words. That's actually the whole strategy in miniature — the fix is to make sure the words exist, whether you write them or someone else does. Relying on a third party to accidentally transcribe your menu is not a plan; writing it in text yourself is.

The second is that vision-capable models genuinely are improving, and the gap between what AI can see and what it does see when recommending you will narrow over time. I don't want to tell you images will be invisible forever, because that's probably not true. But planning your visibility around a capability that isn't reliably in the recommendation pipeline today is a bet against your own interests. Write the text now; let improving vision be a bonus you didn't need, rather than a crutch you depended on.

Where this bites hardest, and a practical fix list

Some businesses are far more exposed than others. Restaurants that publish menus as images, product sellers whose specs live in graphics, and local businesses whose hours and offers are rendered into banners are the ones I see losing the most, because the information most likely to be asked about is exactly the information they've hidden. If that's you, this is some of the highest-leverage AI-visibility work available, precisely because it's so mechanical.

Here's the practical pass I'd run. Go through your important pages and, for each image, ask one question: is there any information in this picture that a customer might ask an AI about? If yes, make sure that information also exists as real, crawlable text on the page — in a caption, a paragraph, or a proper text version of the menu, list or table. Write alt text that describes the actual content rather than labeling the file. Prioritize the pages tied to buying decisions — menus, pricing, products, services, contact and hours — over decorative or gallery pages where the image is the point and carries no facts.

Then verify it the way the model would experience it. Ask an AI about the specifics that live in your images — "what's on the menu at," "how much does X cost at," "what are the specs of" — and see whether it can answer. If it can't, or it guesses, you've confirmed the information is still trapped in pixels, and you know exactly where to go add the text. My read is that for a lot of businesses, this is the single most underrated gap in AI visibility: not a subtle authority problem, but half their website sitting in a format the recommending systems can't read — and the fix is as simple as saying in words what the picture already shows.

Key takeaways

  • For practical purposes, assume AI recommendation systems read your site's text, not your images — if a fact exists only inside a picture, treat it as invisible to the systems deciding who gets named.
  • There's a gap between what a page looks like to a human and what it says to a machine: a beautiful menu, price list or spec sheet rendered as an image can read as a nearly empty page.
  • The information most often hidden in pixels — menus, prices, specs, comparison tables, infographics, hours — is exactly what buyers ask AI about, so the loss lands on high-intent queries.
  • Fix it with three stacking layers: real alt text that describes the content, captions and nearby body text that restate the facts in words, and a true text version wherever the information actually matters.
  • Restaurants with photo menus, sellers with spec graphics, and local businesses with offers baked into banners are the most exposed — and have the most to gain from this mechanical fix.
  • Verify by asking an AI about the specifics that live in your images; if it can't answer or guesses, the information is still trapped in pixels and you know exactly where to add the text.

Frequently asked questions

Can't modern AI models just look at images with vision capabilities?
Some can, in the sense that a vision-capable model shown an image can often describe it. But that's a different question from whether the systems building AI recommendations are actually reading your site's images at the moment they decide who to cite. In practice, the pipelines behind grounded AI answers are built around crawling and quoting text, and an image on your page is mostly a file reference to them. My deliberately conservative rule is to assume information that exists only as an image doesn't exist for AI recommendations. You might occasionally be pleasantly surprised, but you shouldn't plan around the surprise — the cost of assuming it's invisible and adding text anyway is tiny, and the cost of assuming it's readable when it isn't is lost visibility you never notice.
What makes good alt text for AI visibility, versus the alt text I already have?
Most alt text is a label — 'menu,' 'our storefront,' or worse, the filename. That tells a machine a picture exists but not what it says. Useful alt text describes the actual information the image carries: instead of 'our menu,' it conveys what's on the menu; instead of 'price chart,' it states what the prices are. That said, alt text is only the most direct layer. The more powerful move is putting the same information in captions and body text near the image, because crawlable body text is what AI pipelines read most reliably. Think of alt text as the minimum and real on-page text as the goal — and keep the image itself for the humans who still have to choose you.
Do I have to stop using images and infographics, then?
No — and you shouldn't, because images do real work for the humans who ultimately decide whether to buy after AI names you. The point isn't to remove visuals, it's to stop letting them be the only place important information lives. Keep the beautifully designed menu, the infographic, the product photos. Just make sure every fact a customer might ask an AI about also exists as real text on the page — in a caption, a paragraph, or a proper text version of the menu or table. You're adding a readable layer underneath the visual one, not choosing between them. The infographic is actually the sharpest example: it concentrates your best, most quotable information into the one format AI reads worst, so pairing it with a plain-text summary is pure upside.
