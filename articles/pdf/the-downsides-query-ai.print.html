The Downsides Query: How AI Answers “What Are the Cons of Working With You?”

By Scott Tischler · November 18, 2026 · 9 min read

When a serious prospect asks an AI assistant "what are the downsides of working with [you]?", the model answers — whether or not you've ever said a word about your own limitations. If you've stayed silent, that answer gets assembled from scattered complaints, competitor comparisons, and generic category cons that may not even apply to you. The businesses that win this query are the ones that have published their own honest limitations first, because when you name your trade-offs clearly, the model tends to repeat your framing instead of inventing a worse one.

There's a whole family of questions people now type into AI before they buy — the pricing question, the "is it worth it" question, the comparison, the alternatives, the switching question. The downsides query is the one almost nobody optimizes for, and it might be the most revealing of the set. It's what a prospect asks when they're close to deciding and doing their due diligence. By the time someone is asking an AI for your cons, they're interested enough to be looking for a reason not to proceed. The answer they get shapes whether they ever contact you at all.

The query you're not optimizing for

Here's what makes this one different from the flattering queries. When someone asks "who's the best provider for X," you want to be named. That's the obvious game, and most AEO effort goes there. But the downsides query is adversarial by design. The person is explicitly asking the model to argue against you. And the model will comply — it will produce cons, because that's what was asked, and because a refusal to name any downside would itself read as unhelpful or evasive.

So the question is never whether AI will say something negative about you. It will. The question is whether the negative it surfaces is accurate, fair, and framed the way you'd frame it — or whether it's a distortion assembled from the loudest complaint it could find and a competitor's talking point. Those two outcomes produce very different buying decisions, and the difference between them is largely determined by whether you got there first with your own honest version.

Most businesses have never published a single sentence acknowledging a real limitation. Every page is a pitch. So when the model needs to answer a cons question, there's nothing from the business itself to draw on, and it reaches elsewhere. You ceded the most consequential question in the funnel without realizing it was being asked.

Where AI gets your "cons" when you stay silent

When you've published nothing about your own trade-offs, the model fills the vacuum from whatever's available. In my experience watching these answers get built, that's usually some mix of a few sources, none of them ones you'd choose.

  • Your loudest negative reviews. Not your typical experience — your most extreme one. A single vivid complaint can become the model's headline "con," weighted far out of proportion to how representative it is, simply because it's specific and emotionally legible.
  • Competitor and comparison content. Plenty of pages exist specifically to position against businesses like yours, and some of them rank and get cited. If a competitor's "why we're better than [you]" page is the most structured take on your downsides, the model may lift its framing as if it were neutral.
  • Generic category cons. Absent anything specific, the model falls back on the stock criticisms of your entire category — "can be expensive," "results vary," "requires commitment" — and attaches them to you whether or not they're your actual weak points. You get tarred with the industry's reputation rather than judged on your own.
  • Inference from what's missing. If every competitor publishes something you don't — a guarantee, transparent pricing, a certain certification — the model can frame that absence as a downside, even when there's a perfectly good reason for it that you simply never explained.

None of these is under your control, and all of them are worse than the honest truth you could have told. That's the core insight: staying silent doesn't keep the downsides out of the answer. It just guarantees the version in the answer isn't yours.

It's worth sitting with how asymmetric this is. The flattering queries — "who's the best," "who should I use" — are ones you're competing to win against many rivals, and losing one just means someone else gets named. The downsides query is different: here you're not competing with rivals, you're competing with the worst available account of yourself, and losing means a prospect who was already interested talks themselves out of you. The downside answer reaches people deep in the funnel, which makes each distorted one disproportionately expensive. That's why I'd argue this query deserves attention out of proportion to how often it's asked.

The counterintuitive move: publish your own downsides

The fix runs against every marketing instinct, which is exactly why so few businesses do it and why it works. You publish your own honest limitations, in your own words, somewhere on your site a model can read. Who you're not the right fit for. The trade-offs that come with how you operate. The situations where a prospect would be better served by someone else.

Two things happen when you do this. First, you give the model a credible, first-party source for the cons question, and models tend to prefer a clear, direct, well-structured answer over a vague one assembled from fragments. When your honest framing is the most extractable version available, it's the one that gets repeated. You don't eliminate the negative — you author it.

Second, and this is the part that surprises people, honestly naming who you're not for is one of the strongest trust signals you can send, to the model and to the human reading its answer. A business that can clearly articulate its own limitations reads as confident and self-aware rather than defensive. Counterintuitively, "here's who shouldn't work with us" makes the people who should work with you trust you more. The AI picks up on that framing, and the qualified prospect does too.

I'll be candid that this takes nerve, and it isn't a trick — it only works because it's true. You're not writing fake, flattering "downsides" like "our only flaw is we care too much." Models and people both see through that instantly, and it reads worse than saying nothing. The move is to name real trade-offs that genuinely don't matter to your ideal customer while mattering a lot to the people you don't want anyway.

How to do it without torching your pitch

The goal is to shape the cons answer, not to talk yourself out of the sale. There's a craft to naming limitations in a way that qualifies rather than repels, and it comes down to which trade-offs you choose to be honest about.

The ones to name are the trade-offs that are features to the right customer and dealbreakers to the wrong one. If you're premium, "we're not the cheapest option" is a limitation that actively attracts the buyer who associates price with quality and repels the bargain-hunter who was going to be a painful client anyway. If you're specialized, "we only do X, so if you need a one-stop shop we're not it" sends the generalists elsewhere and signals depth to the person who wants a specialist. If you require real commitment, saying so filters out the people who'd have churned and reassures the serious ones. In each case the honest con is doing qualification work — pushing away poor-fit prospects while making good-fit prospects trust you more.

Where you put it matters too. An "Is [us] right for you?" section, a frank FAQ entry answering "what are the downsides," an about page that names your trade-offs plainly — these give the model clean, clearly-labeled text to pull from when the downsides query comes in. Frame each limitation as the flip side of a real strength, state it directly enough to be quotable, and let it stand without burying it in defensiveness. State the trade-off, state who it matters to, and move on.

One caution on tone, because it's where this goes wrong. There's a difference between acknowledging a trade-off and apologizing for your business. "We're a specialist firm, so we're not the right call if you need everything under one roof" is a confident boundary. "Unfortunately we're quite limited and can't do much" is self-sabotage that the model will happily quote right back. Same underlying fact, opposite effect. Write your limitations the way a confident expert would say them out loud to a prospect they respect — plainly, without flinching, and without groveling. The model mirrors the register it finds.

Then go ask the engines yourself. Put your own business into ChatGPT, Gemini, and Perplexity and ask for the downsides of working with you. Whatever comes back is the answer your prospects are already getting. If it's unfair, distorted, or lifted from your worst review, that's not a reputation you're stuck with — it's a gap in what you've published. Fill it with the honest version, and over time the model has something better to reach for. The downsides query is going to get answered either way. The only real choice is whether you're the author.

Frequently asked questions

Isn't publishing my own downsides just giving prospects reasons not to buy?
It feels that way, but the downsides are already in the AI answer your prospects see — you're not introducing them, you're correcting them. The choice is between your accurate, well-framed version and a distortion assembled from your worst review and a competitor's positioning. And naming who you're not for actually builds trust with the people who are a good fit, because self-awareness reads as confidence. Done right, honest limitations qualify prospects rather than repel the ones you want.
What kind of limitations should I actually publish?
The ones that are a feature to your ideal customer and a dealbreaker to the wrong one. 'We're not the cheapest' attracts quality-focused buyers and repels bargain-hunters. 'We only specialize in X' sends generalists elsewhere and signals depth. 'We require real commitment' filters out people who'd have churned. Avoid fake humble-brag downsides like 'we care too much' — models and people both see through those, and they read worse than saying nothing.
How do I know what AI currently says are my downsides?
Ask it directly. Put your business into ChatGPT, Gemini, and Perplexity and ask for the downsides or cons of working with you. Whatever comes back is roughly what your prospects are already being told during their due diligence. If it's unfair or lifted from a single bad review, that's a gap in what you've published rather than a fixed reputation — and you close it by publishing the honest version in clear, quotable text on your own site.
