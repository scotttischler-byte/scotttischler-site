Correct the Record: How to Fix a Wrong Fact AI States About You

By Scott Tischler · November 27, 2026 · 9 min read

If an AI assistant states something wrong about your business — the wrong price, a service you don't offer, a claim you never made, a location you closed years ago — the fix is almost never to argue with the model. You can't edit it, and telling it "that's incorrect" in a chat changes nothing for the next person who asks. What you actually change is the material the model reads: you find the sources carrying the wrong fact, correct or outweigh them with clear, consistent, true information you control, and then wait for the models to re-read the web. It's slower and less satisfying than a delete button, but it's the only lever that works.

I get asked about this constantly, usually with real frustration, because a wrong AI answer feels personal in a way a wrong search result never did. A search result is one blue link among ten that you can scroll past. An AI answer is stated as fact, in confident prose, as if the model checked. So when it says your pricing starts at a number you abandoned two years ago, or lists a service you stopped offering, or — worse — repeats a competitor's talking point about you, it lands like an accusation. The instinct is to find the "report a problem" button and demand a correction. My honest read is that chasing that button is mostly wasted effort. The durable fix lives upstream, in the sources, and that's where this playbook spends its time.

Why you can't just tell the AI it's wrong

Start with how these systems actually produce an answer, because it explains why the obvious move fails. A model doesn't hold a tidy, editable record of your business that a support ticket can patch. It generates each answer fresh, pulling from patterns in its training data and, increasingly, from live sources it retrieves at the moment of the question. When it says something false about you, that falsehood is coming from somewhere — a stale page, an old directory listing, a third-party write-up, a cached version of your own site, or a confident guess stitched from your category. The chat is the output, not the source. Correcting the output in one conversation doesn't touch whatever produced it.

This is also why the same wrong fact tends to show up across ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, and Google's AI answers at once. People assume they're dealing with one system's bug. Usually they're dealing with one bad source that all of them happen to be reading. That's actually good news: it means the problem is centralized. If four different assistants all say the same wrong thing, there's a strong chance one or two findable sources are feeding all of them, and fixing those addresses the whole set rather than four separate complaints.

Some platforms do offer feedback mechanisms, and where they exist I'd use them — a thumbs-down, a correction form, a verified-business process. But I treat them as a minor, supplementary move, not the plan. They're opaque, slow, and offer no guarantee anything changes. Betting your reputation on a feedback form is betting on a black box. The reliable work is making the true version of the fact so clear, consistent, and well-corroborated across the open web that the model has no good reason to reach for the wrong one.

Find where the wrong fact actually lives

Before you can fix anything, you have to locate the source, and this is the step most people skip in their rush to be upset. The wrong fact is being pulled from somewhere specific. Your job is to find the somewhere.

Work it like an investigation. A few places to look first:

  • Your own site, including the pages you forgot about. This is the most common culprit and the most fixable. An old pricing page that still ranks, a blog post from three years ago, a landing page you never took down, a cached description in your footer. Models read your whole site, not just the page you updated. If any corner of it still states the old fact, you've handed the model a contradiction and let it pick.
  • Directories and third-party listings. Your Google Business Profile, Yelp, industry directories, aggregator databases, old press mentions. These get scraped and trusted heavily, and they're notorious for carrying facts you changed long ago — an old phone number, a former address, outdated hours, a service you dropped.
  • Ask the AI where it got it. On systems that cite or browse, ask the model directly: what source are you using for that? It won't always answer cleanly, and on models that don't retrieve live it may not know, but on Perplexity and the browsing modes it will often hand you the exact page. That's your target.
  • The confident-guess case. Sometimes there's no single wrong source — the model has little specific information about you and is filling the gap with the generic pattern of your category. There's nothing to delete here. The fix is the opposite: publish enough clear, specific, true information that the model stops guessing because it finally has something real to use.

Run the prompts that surfaced the error a few different ways and watch what sources come up. You're building a short list of the pages carrying the falsehood. Everything downstream depends on having that list, because you can't overwrite a source you haven't identified.

The correction playbook: overwrite the sources, don't argue with the model

Once you know where the wrong fact lives, the work is methodical. The principle underneath all of it: you're not persuading the model, you're changing what it reads, so that the true fact is the easiest, most consistent, best-corroborated thing available.

  • Fix everything you own first. This is the fastest, highest-leverage move and it's entirely in your control. Correct the fact everywhere on your own site — and I mean everywhere, including the stale pages, the old posts, the footer, the fine print. Delete or update what's outdated. The goal is that your own properties state the true fact consistently and state nothing that contradicts it. A single lingering old page can keep the wrong answer alive, so be thorough rather than quick here.
  • Correct the third-party sources you can reach. Update your Google Business Profile, claim and fix directory listings, request corrections where a site lets you. Many directories have an edit or claim process; use it. This is tedious, unglamorous cleanup, and it's exactly the kind of thing that quietly moves the answer.
  • Where you can't edit, outweigh. Some sources won't let you change them — an old article, a third-party write-up, someone else's page. You can't force a correction, so instead you make the true fact dominant everywhere else. If one old article says the wrong thing and ten current, authoritative sources say the right thing clearly, the weight of evidence shifts, and models tend to follow the consistent majority over a lone outdated outlier.
  • State the correction explicitly and in plain language. Don't just quietly change a number. Where it fits, publish content that names the current reality directly — "as of this year, we offer X and no longer offer Y" — in the natural phrasing someone would use to ask. Explicit, dated, unambiguous statements are easier for a model to lift and trust than a silent edit it has to infer.
  • Add corroboration, not just assertion. Your own say-so is one signal. The true fact, echoed across your profile, your listings, a recent press mention, a partner's page, becomes something the model sees confirmed from multiple independent directions. Corroboration is what moves a fact from "claimed" to "established," and it's usually the difference between a correction that sticks and one that gets overridden by the next stale source.

Notice that none of this is a message to the AI. It's housekeeping on the open web. That's the uncomfortable truth of correcting the record: the work is boring, distributed, and entirely about sources, not about the chat where you saw the problem.

What to expect: timelines, limits, and when it won't fully clear

I want to set honest expectations, because this is where people get discouraged. Fixing the sources does not fix the answer overnight. Models re-read the web on their own schedules — live-retrieval systems update faster, but models relying on training data may carry an old fact until a future update. You can do everything right and still see the wrong answer for weeks. That lag is real, it's frustrating, and there's no way to force it. The move is to get the sources clean and correct as fast as you can, then let the re-reading catch up, checking periodically rather than refreshing in despair.

There are also limits worth naming. If the wrong fact is widely repeated across many sources you don't control, a few corrections won't flip it immediately — you're shifting a weight of evidence, and that takes volume and time. If the falsehood originated from a single stubborn high-authority page, that one source can keep reasserting itself until it's corrected or clearly outweighed. And if the "wrong" fact is actually a matter of interpretation — a characterization, an opinion from a review, a framing you dislike rather than a factual error — that's a different problem than a wrong number, and it rarely has a clean fix at all. Be clear with yourself about which one you're dealing with, because a plain factual error is correctable and a reputation you don't like is a different, longer piece of work.

The practical sequence is simple even when the work isn't: find the sources, fix what you own, correct what you can reach, outweigh what you can't, state the truth explicitly and get it corroborated, then wait and re-check. Skip straight to the feedback form and you'll mostly spin. Do the source work and you're pulling the one lever that actually moves these systems. It's slower than anyone wants, and it's the honest answer — the wrong fact lives in the material the model reads, so the material is where you fix it.

Key takeaways

  • You can't edit an AI model or argue a wrong answer out of it in a chat — the chat is the output, not the source. The fix lives in the material the model reads.
  • The same wrong fact usually appears across ChatGPT, Gemini, and Perplexity at once because they're all reading one or two bad sources — which means one fix can address all of them.
  • Find where the falsehood actually lives first: stale pages on your own site, outdated directory listings, third-party write-ups, or a confident guess filling an information gap.
  • Fix everything you own first (including the old pages you forgot about), correct the third-party sources you can reach, and outweigh the ones you can't with consistent, corroborated truth.
  • State the correction explicitly and in plain language, and back it with corroboration from multiple independent sources — that's what moves a fact from 'claimed' to 'established.'
  • Expect a lag: models re-read the web on their own schedule, so a clean fix can still show the old answer for weeks. There's no button to force it — get the sources right, then wait and re-check.
