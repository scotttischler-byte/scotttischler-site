What Your Internal Links Tell AI About What You Actually Do

By Scott Tischler · November 21, 2026 · 9 min read

Internal links do something most business owners never think about: they tell an answer engine which of your pages is the authoritative answer to a given question, and the words you use to link — the anchor text — tell it what that page is about. If your most important service page is linked to from three places using the words "click here," "learn more," and "our services," you've handed AI almost nothing to work with. If it's linked from twenty places using the exact phrase a customer would type, you've drawn a clear map straight to it. This is one of the cheapest, most-overlooked levers in AI visibility, and it lives entirely on your own site, under your own control.

I want to be careful here, because internal linking has a long and slightly tired history in SEO, and a lot of what was written about it over the years was about chasing Google's algorithm in ways that aged badly. The reason it matters again — differently — is that answer engines don't read your site the way a person does. They don't land on your homepage and intuit the structure. They retrieve passages, follow links, and build a model of what you do from how your pages connect and what they say about each other. The internal link is one of the few places where you get to narrate that model in your own words. Most sites waste the chance.

Why machines read links differently than people do

When a human clicks a link that says "learn more," context fills in the rest. They can see the sentence around it, the heading above it, the image beside it. They know what "more" refers to. A retrieval system pulling your content apart into chunks often doesn't have that luxury. The anchor text — the clickable words themselves — is frequently the clearest, most portable signal about where that link goes and why. It travels with the link. It's a label the machine can read without needing the whole page around it.

So the words you choose to link with are doing double duty. To a person, they're a prompt to click. To an answer engine, they're a description of the destination. "Learn more about our emergency plumbing service" and "click here" point to the same page, but only one of them tells the machine that the page is about emergency plumbing. Multiply that across every link on your site and you start to see the problem. A site linked together with vague, generic anchors is a site that has refused to describe itself. A site linked together with specific, descriptive anchors is one that explains, over and over, in consistent language, exactly what each page is for.

There's a second thing machines read that people ignore entirely: how many times, and from where, a given page gets linked internally. The pages you link to most often, from your most important pages, read as your most important pages. That's a signal about hierarchy — what you consider central versus peripheral. If your deepest, best service page is buried three clicks down and linked from nowhere, while a thin blog post from 2022 is linked in your main navigation, you've told the machine the blog post matters more. It will often believe you.

The signal most sites are actually sending

Walk your own site the way a crawler would and you'll usually find one of a few failure patterns. The first is the generic-anchor problem I've described — "read more," "here," "this page," "contact us" — repeated everywhere, labeling nothing. The second is the orphan problem: important pages that aren't linked from anywhere except the sitemap, which means the machine may reach them but gets no signal about what they relate to or how much they matter. The third, and sneakiest, is the inconsistency problem: the same page described five different ways by five different links, so the machine never gets a stable read on what it's about.

That last one deserves a pause, because it's the one even careful sites get wrong. Say you offer a specific service — let's call it commercial roof repair. One link calls it "roofing," another "commercial services," another "repairs," another "what we do for businesses." Each is reasonable. Together they dilute the signal. The machine sees a page that's vaguely about roofing, or services, or repairs, and never gets the concentrated, repeated phrase "commercial roof repair" that would let it confidently match that page to the query. Consistency isn't about keyword stuffing. It's about not scattering your own signal across four near-synonyms when one clear phrase, used consistently, would have landed.

The deeper issue underneath all three patterns is that most internal linking happens by accident. Links get added when someone remembers to, with whatever words were convenient in the moment. Nobody is treating the link as a description. And because it works fine for human visitors — who fill in the gaps — the cost stays invisible. It only shows up when a machine, which can't fill in the gaps, tries to figure out which of your pages is the answer and finds no clear signal pointing anywhere.

How to link so AI understands what you do

The fix isn't technical and it isn't expensive. It's editorial. A handful of habits, applied consistently, change the signal your whole site sends.

  • Link with descriptive anchor text, not generic filler. When you link to a page, use words that describe what's on the other end — ideally the phrase a customer would actually use for it. "Our commercial roof repair process" instead of "learn more." The anchor should be able to stand alone as a label and still make sense. If you stripped away the surrounding sentence, would the link's words still tell you where it goes? If not, rewrite them.
  • Pick one phrase per page and use it consistently. Decide what each important page is about, in the words a buyer would use, and link to it that way across your site. You can vary naturally — you're writing for humans too — but the core phrase should repeat. Consistency concentrates the signal instead of scattering it.
  • Link your best pages from your most important pages. The pages that actually make you money or answer real buyer questions should be well-linked from your homepage, your main service pages, and your most-read posts. Internal link volume from strong pages is a hierarchy signal. Make your priorities match the machine's read of them.
  • Kill the orphans. Find pages that aren't linked from anywhere meaningful and connect them to related content. A page with no internal links pointing to it is a page you've told the machine not to care about, whatever its quality.
  • Link related content to build topic clusters. When several pages cover facets of the same subject, link them to each other with descriptive anchors. This tells the machine these pages belong together and that, collectively, you go deep on the topic — which is exactly the topical-authority signal answer engines reward. A cluster of interlinked pages on one subject reads as expertise. A scatter of unlinked pages reads as noise.

None of this requires a developer. It's reading your own content, deciding what each page is for in plain customer language, and then linking to it in those words, consistently, from the places that matter. The work is in the deciding, not the doing.

What internal links can and can't do

I want to be honest about the ceiling here, because internal linking gets oversold the moment anyone notices it works. Internal links are a signal about your own site's structure and meaning. They are not a substitute for being talked about elsewhere. A perfectly interlinked site that no one off your domain ever mentions is still a weak entity in the eyes of an answer engine — the corroboration that moves the needle most comes from third parties, not from how cleverly you've linked your own pages together. Internal links help the machine understand and prioritize what you've got. They don't manufacture authority you haven't earned.

What they do reliably is make sure that when the authority is there, it isn't wasted on a muddled structure. Think of it this way: everything else you do — the content, the reviews, the mentions, the proof — points at your site. Internal linking decides whether, once a machine arrives, it can tell which page is the answer and what that page is about. You can do all the hard work of earning attention and then fumble the handoff because your own site refused to describe itself clearly. That's the failure internal linking prevents, and it's a common one.

My read, and it's a read rather than a guarantee, is that this lever is going to matter more over time, not less, because answer engines are increasingly building structured understandings of entities and their content, and the internal link is one of the clearest in-your-control inputs to that understanding. The practical move is unglamorous: audit your important pages, look at how they're linked and with what words, and fix the anchors that describe nothing and the pages that connect to nothing. It's an afternoon of editorial work that quietly changes what every machine crawling your site concludes about what you actually do.

Key takeaways

  • Anchor text — the clickable words — is a label machines read without the surrounding page; 'click here' and 'learn more' describe nothing, so they teach AI nothing about where the link goes.
  • How often and from where a page gets linked internally is a hierarchy signal: your best pages should be well-linked from your strongest pages, or AI will rank your priorities differently than you do.
  • The sneakiest failure is inconsistency — describing the same page five different ways scatters your signal; pick one customer-phrase per page and use it consistently.
  • Orphan pages (linked from nowhere meaningful) read as pages you've told the machine not to care about, no matter how good they are.
  • Linking related pages into clusters with descriptive anchors signals topical depth — the authority answer engines reward — while a scatter of unlinked pages reads as noise.
  • Internal links are a self-signal, not a source of authority: they make sure earned attention isn't wasted on a muddled structure, but they can't manufacture credibility you haven't built off-site.

Frequently asked questions

Isn't internal linking just old-school SEO that doesn't matter anymore?
The version that chased Google's algorithm with keyword-stuffed anchors aged badly, and I'm not reviving that. What's changed is that answer engines retrieve and reassemble your content in pieces, and the internal link is one of the few portable, in-your-control signals that tells a machine which page answers which question and what it's about. So the tactic is old but the reason is new — it's about helping a retrieval system understand your site, not gaming a ranking. Done as plain editorial work rather than manipulation, it still matters, arguably more.
What's the single most impactful change I can make to my internal links?
Replace generic anchors with descriptive ones, using the phrase a customer would actually say. If your key pages are currently linked with 'learn more,' 'here,' and 'our services,' changing those to specific descriptions of what's on the other end — consistently, across the site — is the highest-leverage fix. It costs nothing but attention, and it turns every link from a blank label into a description the machine can read. Start with the pages that actually drive your business and work outward.
Will better internal linking get AI to recommend me if I'm not being mentioned elsewhere?
On its own, probably not, and I'd be lying if I said otherwise. Internal linking is a signal about your own site's structure and meaning — it helps a machine understand and prioritize what you've published. But the authority that actually moves AI recommendations comes largely from corroboration off your domain: who talks about you, cites you, and lists you. Internal linking makes sure that when that authority exists, your site cleanly communicates which page is the answer. Treat it as the thing that stops you fumbling the handoff, not as a source of credibility by itself.
