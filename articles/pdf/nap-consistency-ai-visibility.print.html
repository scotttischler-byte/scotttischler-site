Does NAP Consistency Still Matter for AI? Why Conflicting Business Facts Quietly Cost You Recommendations

By Scott Tischler · November 26, 2026 · 8 min read

Yes — keeping your core business facts consistent across the web still matters, and in the AI era it may matter more than it did for old-school local SEO, but the reason has changed. AI isn't tallying up how many directories list you the way a local-search ranking once did. It's building a confidence picture of who you are, where you are, and what you do, by reading everything it can find about you and looking for agreement. When your name, address, phone, hours, categories or services say different things in different places, you don't just lose a ranking point — you lower the model's confidence that it knows you well enough to recommend you at all. This is my read as a practitioner, not a claim about any model's internals, but the pattern is consistent across the businesses I look at.

For years, "NAP consistency" — name, address, phone — was a local SEO chore. You made sure your listings matched, you cleaned up duplicates, and you treated it as a box to check. The box is still there, but the machine reading those facts is different now, and it's asking a different question. Old search asked "how many trustworthy sources confirm this business exists here?" AI asks "do I actually know who this business is, and am I confident enough to put my name behind recommending it?" Those sound similar. They're not, and the difference is what this article is about.

From citation count to confidence

The old model rewarded volume. More consistent citations across more directories nudged you up the local pack, and the work was largely about getting listed in as many places as possible with matching details. Consistency helped, but a few mismatches among a hundred listings rarely sank you.

AI works differently because it's not ranking a list — it's deciding whether to name you in a sentence, and it has to resolve you to a single, coherent entity before it can do that. When a model assembles what it knows about your business, it's effectively reconciling a pile of statements: this source says you're on Main Street, this one says you moved to Oak Avenue, this one has a phone number that now rings a different company, this one calls you a "spa," this one calls you a "wellness clinic." The model's job is to collapse all of that into one confident answer. When the statements agree, that collapse is easy and the model comes away confident. When they conflict, the model is left holding contradictions, and contradictions read as uncertainty.

Uncertainty is expensive in an AI answer, because the model is staking a bit of its own credibility every time it recommends someone. Faced with a business it isn't sure it has pinned down correctly, the safe move is to hedge, to describe you vaguely, or to quietly pick a competitor it understands better. You rarely see this happen — there's no error message — but in my experience it's one of the most common and least noticed ways businesses lose AI visibility.

What actually counts as a "fact" now

NAP was always shorthand. The facts AI is reconciling about you go well beyond name, address and phone, and the broader set is where most of the damage hides.

  • The identity trio — name, address, phone. Still foundational, because they're how the model matches a mention to you specifically rather than to a business with a similar name. A phone number that now belongs to someone else, or an address you left two years ago, doesn't just look messy — it can cause the model to attach the wrong signals to you, or to split you into two half-formed entities.
  • Hours, service area and "open/closed" status. When AI answers "who's open near me" or "who serves my area," conflicting hours or a listing that still shows a closed location gives it a reason to leave you out of exactly the high-intent queries you most want.
  • What you are — category and services. If half your footprint calls you one thing and half calls you another, the model struggles to decide which queries you even belong in. This is subtler than a wrong phone number but just as costly.
  • Ownership, brand and relationships. For businesses that have rebranded, merged, changed owners or run under a DBA, the web often carries several overlapping versions. The model has to figure out which are the same entity and which aren't, and it doesn't always get it right.

The through-line is that every one of these is a statement the model has to reconcile, and every unresolved conflict is a small withdrawal from the confidence account. Individually they're minor. In aggregate they decide whether the model feels it knows you.

Where the conflicts come from

Almost no business creates these contradictions on purpose. They accumulate. Understanding the usual sources makes them much easier to hunt down.

Moves and number changes you never fully propagated. You updated your own site and your Google profile when you moved, but the old address and the old phone number live on in dozens of directories, aggregators and old articles you forgot existed. Those don't expire on their own. They keep feeding the model your past.

Auto-generated listings you never created. Large data aggregators generate business profiles automatically, often with stale or slightly-wrong details pulled from who-knows-where. You may have listings you've never seen, saying things you'd never say, and the model reads them right alongside the ones you control.

Rebrands and repositioning. You changed your name, your category or your whole model, but the web still describes the previous version. The model faithfully reports what it finds, which is a blend of old and new that adds up to confusion.

Inconsistent self-description. Sometimes the conflict is entirely your own doing — your website calls you one thing on the homepage and another on the about page, your social profiles each describe you a little differently, and your own house isn't telling one story. If you can't stay consistent about yourself, you can't expect the model to.

Why small and local businesses get hit hardest

This problem scales inversely with how much control you have over your own footprint, which is why it lands hardest on exactly the businesses least equipped to notice it. A large national brand has a communications team, a tightly managed set of official profiles, and enough authoritative coverage that the correct version of its facts drowns out any stragglers. The consensus is so strong that a few stale records barely register.

A small or local business is the opposite. Its footprint is thinner, so each individual source carries more relative weight — one wrong aggregator listing isn't one voice among thousands, it's one voice among a few dozen, and it pulls the average meaningfully. These businesses also tend to have the messiest histories from the model's point of view: they've moved, changed phone numbers, swapped owners, run under a couple of different names, and never had the resources to chase down every listing when they did. The result is that the businesses most dependent on being found locally are often the ones whose facts are most scattered.

There's a flip side worth sitting with, though, and it's encouraging. Because a small business has a thinner footprint, a focused cleanup can move its consensus faster than a big brand could ever move its own. You don't have ten thousand sources to reconcile — you have a manageable set, and getting the prominent ones to agree can meaningfully sharpen how confidently the model knows you, sometimes within a single cleanup pass. The leverage runs in your favor once you actually do the work.

How to audit and fix it

The good news is that this is one of the more fixable parts of AI visibility, because it's concrete. You're not guessing at an algorithm — you're making the world's description of you agree with itself.

Start by deciding, in writing, the single canonical version of your facts: exact business name, address, phone, hours, primary category, and the two or three service descriptions you want associated with you. This is your source of truth, and everything else gets measured against it. Most consistency problems trace back to never having defined this clearly.

Then get your own house in order first, because it's the part you fully control and the model weights it heavily. Make your website, your about page, your contact page and your own profiles all tell the exact same story in the same words. If your own surfaces disagree, fix that before you touch anything external.

Next, go find the external conflicts. Search your business name, old addresses and old phone numbers, and see what comes back. Claim and correct the major profiles and aggregators you can, and prioritize the ones that show up most prominently when you search yourself — those are the ones the model is most likely weighting. You won't scrub every stale mention off the internet, and you don't need to. You need the recent, prominent, high-trust consensus to agree, so that it outweighs the scattered old contradictions.

Finally, audit it the way the model experiences it: ask the major AI engines who you are, where you're located, what you do and whether you're open, and read what they say back. If they get your details wrong, hedge, or describe an old version of you, you've found exactly which conflicts still outweigh the truth — and you now know which consensus you need to go rebuild. My read is that consistency has quietly shifted from a ranking factor to a trust factor, and trust is the currency AI spends when it decides whether to recommend you. Conflicting facts don't get you penalized so much as they get you passed over, which is harder to notice and every bit as costly.

Key takeaways

  • Consistency of your core business facts still matters, but the job changed: AI isn't counting citations, it's building a confidence picture of who you are and whether it knows you well enough to recommend.
  • Conflicting facts rarely get you penalized outright — they get you passed over, because an uncertain entity is a risky thing for a model to put its name behind.
  • The facts AI reconciles go beyond name, address and phone to hours, service area, category, services, ownership and brand — and every unresolved conflict is a small withdrawal from your trust account.
  • Most conflicts accumulate from moves you never fully propagated, auto-generated listings you never created, rebrands the web hasn't caught up to, and your own surfaces disagreeing with each other.
  • Fix it by defining one canonical version of your facts, making your own site and profiles agree first, then correcting the most prominent external listings so recent consensus outweighs stale contradictions.
  • Audit it the way the model sees it: ask the major AI engines who you are, where you are and what you do, and rebuild the consensus wherever they get it wrong.

Frequently asked questions

Is NAP consistency still a ranking factor, or does it only matter for AI now?
It still helps traditional local search, so the cleanup work isn't wasted on either front. But its role has shifted. In classic local SEO, consistency was mostly about accumulating matching citations to support a ranking. For AI, it's about resolving you to a single, coherent entity the model is confident it understands. A few mismatches among many listings rarely sank a local ranking, but for an AI that's trying to decide whether to name you in a one-sentence answer, contradictions read as uncertainty — and uncertainty is a reason to hedge or pick a competitor it understands better. So the same work now protects a different, higher-stakes outcome.
I moved years ago but old addresses and phone numbers keep showing up. How much does that actually hurt?
More than most people expect, because those stale records don't expire on their own — they keep feeding the model your past. The risk isn't only that AI quotes an old address; it's that conflicting location and contact data can lower the model's overall confidence that it has you pinned down, or in bad cases cause it to treat the old and new versions as two half-formed entities. You don't need to scrub every old mention off the internet, which isn't realistic anyway. You need the recent, prominent, high-trust sources to agree on the current truth so that consensus outweighs the scattered old contradictions.
How do I know if conflicting facts are actually hurting my AI visibility?
Ask the major AI engines directly — who is my business, where are we located, what do we do, are we open — and read what they hand back. If they get your details wrong, describe an old version of you, hedge, or seem unsure, that's your signal that conflicting facts still outweigh the truth in your footprint. Then search your own name, old addresses and old phone numbers to find where the contradictions live, and correct the most prominent sources first. Treat the engines' answers as your report card: you re-run it after cleanup and watch whether their confidence and accuracy improve.
