Publish the Number: How Original Research Makes You the Source AI Cites

Scott TischlerFounder & Chairman, AIrecommend.ai · November 27, 2026 · 9 min read
⤓ Download PDFVisit AIrecommend.ai

One of the most reliable ways to get cited by AI is to become the source of a number nobody else has. When you publish genuine original data — a figure from your own operations, a survey of your customers, a benchmark from work only you do — you stop being one more opinion in a crowded category and become a primary source. Answer engines are hungry for concrete, attributable figures and short on them, so a specific, well-sourced statistic that originates with you is exactly the kind of thing a model reaches for and credits. You don't need a research department to do this. You need to publish a true number that didn't exist before.

I've come to think of original research as one of the highest-return moves in AI visibility, and also one of the least used, because it feels like something only big firms with budgets do. It isn't. Most businesses are sitting on data that no one outside the company has ever seen — what their customers actually do, how long things actually take, what actually works — and almost none of them publish it. That's a gap, and in the age of answer engines, a gap in what you publish is a gap in whether you get cited. The businesses that figure this out early get to own the facts their whole category gets described by.

Why AI is starved for original numbers

Think about what an answer engine is doing when someone asks a question that wants a figure — how much something costs, how long a process takes, how common a problem is, what the typical result looks like. The model wants to give a concrete, credible answer, and concrete answers need numbers with a source behind them. Here's the problem it runs into: most of the web is opinion, summary, and recycled generalities. Everyone says "it varies" and "results differ." Actual data points, traceable to a real source, are comparatively rare. So when a specific, attributable number does exist, it stands out, and the model tends to use it and name where it came from.

That's the whole opportunity in one sentence: there's high demand for original numbers and low supply of them. Most content in any given category is assertion stacked on assertion, everyone making the same claims in slightly different words. A real figure that originates with you breaks that pattern. It gives the model something it can't get elsewhere, and — this is the part that compounds — it gives other writers something to cite too, which means your number spreads across the web as other people reference it, building exactly the kind of corroboration that makes AI trust and repeat a fact. One genuinely useful statistic can end up quoted in a dozen places, all tracing back to you.

Contrast that with the self-promotional numbers businesses usually publish — "10,000 happy customers," "20 years of excellence." Those are claims about yourself, and models treat them with appropriate skepticism because anyone can assert them. Original research is different in kind. It's not a boast about you; it's a finding about the world, measured from a vantage point you happen to have. That's what makes it citable. The model isn't vouching for how great you are. It's using a fact you were in a position to know.

What counts as original research when you're not a research lab

The phrase "original research" scares people off because it sounds like academia. Lower the bar. Original research just means a true, specific finding that didn't exist in public before you published it. You almost certainly have the raw material. A few forms it takes for an ordinary business:

  • Your own operational data, anonymized and aggregated. You know things from running your business that no outsider does — typical timelines, common failure points, how often a particular thing happens, what the realistic range of outcomes looks like. Aggregate it so it's not about any one customer, and you've got a finding. "Across the projects we ran this year, the median time to X was Y" is original research, and it's the exact figure someone asking an answer engine wants.
  • A survey of the people you can reach. You have customers, an email list, an audience. Ask them something specific and useful about your category and report what they said. Even a modest survey, honestly described, produces original data points that exist nowhere else. The key is to be straight about the size and method so the number is credible rather than inflated.
  • A benchmark from work only you do. If you do something repeatedly, you can measure it and publish the benchmark — the typical cost, the usual range, the common distribution. You're turning expertise you already have into a measured, stated figure.
  • An honest analysis of public data through your lens. You don't always have to generate the raw data. Taking public information and analyzing it in a way only your expertise allows — spotting the pattern, running the comparison, drawing the finding — produces something original too, as long as the analysis is genuinely yours and genuinely sound.

The non-negotiable across all of these: the number has to be real. I'll say this as plainly as I can — never fabricate a statistic to look authoritative. It's the fastest way to destroy the exact trust you're trying to build, and when a made-up figure gets contradicted by reality or by other sources, you've taught the web and the models that your numbers can't be relied on. The entire value of original research is that it's true and you can stand behind it. A modest, honest, real number beats an impressive invented one every single time, and it's not close.

How to publish a number so AI can cite it

Having the data isn't enough; you have to publish it in a form a model can find, extract, and attribute. The presentation does real work here. A few things make a finding citable:

  • State the number plainly and up front. Don't bury the figure three paragraphs into a narrative. Lead with it, state it in a clean sentence a model can lift whole — the finding, the number, the unit. The easier it is to extract as a single quotable statement, the more likely it is to show up in an answer.
  • Show your method honestly. Say where the number came from, how big the sample was, what period it covers, what it does and doesn't include. This isn't just integrity — though it is that — it's credibility the model can read. A figure with a visible, sound method behind it is more trustworthy than a bare number, and the honesty about limits makes the whole thing more believable, not less.
  • Give it a home that's meant to be referenced. Put the finding on a clear, stable page — a data page, a report, a study — rather than letting it float in a passing blog aside. You want a canonical source other people can link to and the model can return to. Something built to be cited gets cited.
  • Frame it around the question it answers. Phrase the finding the way people actually ask — if the number answers "how long does this usually take," make that connection explicit. The closer your statement sits to the real query, the more directly a model can map one to the other.
  • Make it easy for humans to cite too. Other people quoting your number is what builds the corroboration that makes models trust it. Clear attribution, a quotable stat, a referenceable page — all of it lowers the friction for a writer to point back at you, and every time they do, your number gets more established.

The aim is a finding that travels. You publish a true, specific number, presented so it's easy to extract and easy to credit, and then it does work for you far beyond the original page — quoted by other writers, returned by answer engines, slowly becoming the fact your category gets described by.

The honest limits

A few caveats, because original research isn't magic and I'd rather you go in clear-eyed. It takes real work — gathering and honestly analyzing data is more effort than writing another opinion post, and there's no getting around that. It also doesn't pay off overnight; a finding earns its citations as it spreads and as models re-read the web, which takes time. And not every number is interesting. A statistic nobody was wondering about won't get cited no matter how cleanly you publish it, so the finding has to actually answer a question people have, in a category where that answer is useful.

There's also a discipline cost most people underestimate: once you publish a number, you own it. If it's wrong, sloppy, or inflated, that follows you, and the same visibility that helps you when the figure is solid hurts you when it isn't. That's a feature, not a bug — it's exactly why real original research carries weight and self-serving claims don't — but it means you have to hold yourself to a genuine standard. My read is that for most businesses the trade is well worth it: a little rigor, applied to data you already have, turns you from one more voice making category-standard claims into a primary source the models cite by name. In a world where everyone is publishing opinions, the business that publishes an honest number is the one that gets quoted.

Key takeaways

  • Answer engines want concrete, attributable figures and the open web is mostly opinion and recycled generalities — so a real, specific number that originates with you stands out and gets cited by name.
  • Original research isn't just for big firms: a true, specific finding that didn't exist in public before you published it counts, and most businesses are sitting on exactly that kind of data.
  • It differs from self-promotional claims like '10,000 happy customers' — those are boasts anyone can assert, while a finding about the world, measured from your vantage point, is what makes it citable.
  • Sources include your own aggregated operational data, a survey of your customers, a benchmark from work only you do, or an honest analysis of public data through your lens — but the number must be real.
  • Publish it so a model can use it: state the figure plainly up front, show your method and sample honestly, give it a stable referenceable home, and frame it around the question it answers.
  • Never fabricate a statistic — a made-up number destroys the exact trust you're building, and once you publish a figure you own it, which is precisely why honest original research carries weight.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a research team or a big budget to publish original research?
No. Original research just means a true, specific finding that didn't exist in public before you published it. Most businesses already have the raw material — aggregated operational data on typical timelines or outcomes, a survey of their email list, or a benchmark from work they do repeatedly. The bar is honesty and specificity, not an academic apparatus.
Why would AI cite my number instead of treating it like another marketing claim?
Because it's a finding about the world, not a boast about you. Self-promotional numbers like '20 years of excellence' are claims anyone can assert, so models treat them skeptically. An original figure measured from a vantage point you happen to have — published with a visible, sound method — is something the model can't get elsewhere and can attribute, which is exactly what makes it citable.
What's the single biggest mistake to avoid with original research?
Fabricating or inflating the number.
