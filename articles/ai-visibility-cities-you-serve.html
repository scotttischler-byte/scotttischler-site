The Cities You Serve but Aren't Based In: Getting Recommended Beyond Your Home Address

Scott TischlerFounder & Chairman, AIrecommend.ai · November 21, 2026 · 9 min read
⤓ Download PDFVisit AIrecommend.ai

If you run a service-area business — one office, one address, but customers across a whole region — there's a good chance AI will only ever recommend you in the city where your office physically sits. Ask an answer engine for a good provider in the town twenty minutes away, one you serve every week, and your name doesn't come up, even though you're a better fit than whoever it names. This isn't a glitch. It's the predictable result of how answer engines tie a business to a location, and the fix is a specific, honest piece of work most businesses in this position have never done. This one matters because so many businesses live here: contractors, law firms, medical practices, agencies, home services, anyone who sells into more places than they're headquartered.

I'll say up front what this isn't. It isn't the multi-location problem — a brand with ten real offices in ten cities, each with its own address and page. That's a different puzzle. This is the harder, more common one: a single location, or even no public storefront at all, serving a territory much larger than the dot on the map where your mail gets delivered. The machine knows where you are. What it doesn't reliably know is everywhere you work. And in AI-mediated local recommendations, where you work is the thing that decides whether you get named.

Why AI pins you to one dot on the map

Answer engines build a picture of your business from the signals available to them, and the loudest geographic signal most businesses send is their single address — on their listing, in their schema, in directories, at the bottom of every page. That address is consistent, structured, and everywhere, so the machine anchors you to it with high confidence. When someone asks for a recommendation "near" a place or "in" a town, the machine reaches for businesses it confidently associates with that place. If the only place it confidently associates you with is your home city, that's the only place you surface.

The towns you actually serve usually have none of that reinforcement. There's no address there, no consistent structured mention, often nothing on your own site that even names the place. So from the machine's point of view, your connection to those towns is invisible. You know you serve them. Your customers there know it. But the signal that would tell an answer engine never got created, because for years it didn't need to — word of mouth and a Google Maps radius did the work. Answer engines don't infer a service radius the way a maps product does. They reason from stated associations, and you never stated them.

There's a trust dimension underneath this too. An answer engine is cautious about recommending a business for a place it has no evidence the business operates in, because recommending the wrong-location provider is a bad answer. So in the absence of signal, the safe move is to stick to the one place it's sure about. Your silence about the rest of your territory reads, to the machine, as an absence of coverage. It's not refusing to recommend you elsewhere out of malice. It genuinely doesn't know you belong there, and it won't guess.

The gap between where you are and where you work

Sit with how costly this is, because it compounds in a quiet way. Every query about a town you serve but aren't based in is a query where a competitor — possibly one who is genuinely based there, possibly one who just did this signaling work — gets named instead of you. These aren't fringe queries. "Good [your service] in [nearby town]" is exactly how people now ask, and increasingly they ask an answer engine instead of scrolling a map. If you serve fifteen towns and AI only vouches for you in one, you're invisible for fourteen-fifteenths of your actual market in the channel that's growing fastest.

And the businesses getting named in those towns aren't necessarily better than you. Often they're just more legible. They happened to publish a page about that area, or get listed in a local directory, or get mentioned in a community forum — and that was enough to create the association you never created. This is the frustrating part and also the hopeful part: the thing deciding these recommendations is frequently not quality but evidence of coverage, and evidence of coverage is something you can produce honestly, because you really do serve these places. You're not inventing a presence. You're documenting one that already exists and that you simply never wrote down.

The honesty line matters, so let me draw it clearly. This strategy is for places you genuinely serve — where you really do show up, do the work, and have real customers. It is not a license to claim a hundred towns you've never set foot in to blanket a region with thin, fake location pages. Answer engines are getting better at discounting exactly that kind of manufactured coverage, and it reads as spam to humans too. The move here is to make your real territory legible, not to fabricate a bigger one.

How to earn visibility across your real territory

The work is to create, for the places you serve, the kind of signal that currently only exists for your home city — so the machine has honest evidence of coverage to reason from. A few things do most of the lifting.

  • Name your service area explicitly, in plain language, on your own site. Don't make the machine infer a radius. State the towns, counties, or regions you serve, in words, somewhere substantive — not buried in a footer. A clear "areas we serve" statement that actually lists the places is the baseline signal, and most service-area businesses don't have one worth reading.
  • Create genuinely useful content tied to the places you serve. Not thin doorway pages that swap the town name and change nothing else — those get discounted and deserve to. Real content: a project you did in that area, considerations specific to that town or region, local conditions that affect your work there. The test is whether the page would be useful to someone in that town even if it never mentioned you twice. If it's only there to catch a keyword, the machine can tell, and so can the reader.
  • Earn mentions and listings in the places you serve. Local directories, community sites, regional publications, and forums for the towns in your territory are where off-site evidence of coverage gets created. A mention of your business in a nearby town's local context does more to associate you with that town than anything on your own site, because it's corroboration rather than self-assertion. This is slower work, but it's the work that actually convinces a cautious machine.
  • Get your real projects and customers in those areas documented. Reviews that mention the town, case studies set there, photos and descriptions of work you did in the area — all of it creates the specific, place-tied evidence that you operate there. A review from a customer in a town you serve is worth a great deal, because it's a third party confirming your coverage in the exact place you want to be recommended.
  • Keep the whole picture consistent. Make sure the service area you claim on your site, in your listings, and in your content all tells the same story. A machine building a model of where you work rewards consistency and gets cautious around contradiction. Decide what your real territory is and reinforce it everywhere, in the same terms.

Notice that none of this is a trick. Every piece of it is documenting something true — you serve these places, you've done work there, you have customers there — that simply never got written down in a form a machine could read. That's why it's durable. You're closing the gap between a reality that exists and a signal that didn't.

What to expect, and what this won't do

I want to set honest expectations, because this is slower than flipping a switch. Your home city will probably always be your strongest association — it has the most reinforcement and that's fine. The realistic goal for the rest of your territory is to go from invisible to credible: to give the machine enough honest evidence that it's willing to name you for a town you serve, especially as the competing signal in that town is often thin. The towns where you do the most work, have the most reviews, and create the most real content will come first. The ones where you have a lighter footprint will lag, which is appropriate — the signal should track the reality.

This also won't overcome a genuinely stronger local competitor in every case, nor should it. If a provider is truly based in a town, deeply embedded there, and well-documented, they may deserve the nod for that town, and no amount of signaling changes who's actually the better local fit. What this fixes is the far more common situation where you're a real, strong option for a place and you're losing to no one in particular — just to your own silence. You're being skipped not because someone beat you but because you never told the machine you were in the running. For a business that genuinely serves a territory, closing that gap is some of the highest-return visibility work available, precisely because so few of your competitors in those outer towns have done it either. The first one in a town to make their real coverage legible often just gets it.

Key takeaways

  • Service-area businesses send one loud geographic signal — their single address — so answer engines confidently tie them to their home city and nowhere else, even across a territory they genuinely serve.
  • This is distinct from the multi-location problem: it's one address (or none) serving many towns, where the machine knows where you ARE but not everywhere you WORK — and where you work decides who gets named.
  • AI won't infer a service radius the way a maps product does; it reasons from stated associations, so an unstated service area reads as an absence of coverage, and it plays it safe by sticking to the one city it's sure of.
  • The competitors getting named in your outer towns often aren't better — just more legible, because they published a page or earned a mention that created the association you never did.
  • The fix is documenting real coverage, not fabricating it: explicit service-area statements, genuinely useful place-tied content (not thin doorway pages), and — most powerfully — off-site mentions and reviews from the towns you serve.
  • Expect your home city to stay strongest and outer towns to follow your real footprint; the big opportunity is that few competitors in those towns have done this either, so the first to make real coverage legible often just wins it.

Frequently asked questions

Can't I just make a landing page for every town I want to rank in?
You can, and it's exactly the move I'd warn you away from doing lazily. Thin 'doorway' pages that swap the town name and change nothing else get discounted by answer engines and read as spam to humans — they can hurt more than help. A page tied to a place earns its keep only if it's genuinely useful to someone there: real projects you did in that area, considerations specific to it, local conditions that affect your work. Make it for places you actually serve, and make each page worth reading on its own. The test is whether it would help a local reader even if it barely mentioned you. If it only exists to catch a keyword, skip it.
What's the single highest-impact thing for getting recommended in a town I'm not based in?
Off-site evidence of coverage — a mention, listing, or review that ties your business to that specific town. It outweighs anything on your own site because it's corroboration rather than self-assertion, and a cautious answer engine trusts a third party confirming you operate somewhere far more than it trusts your own claim. A review from a customer in that town, a listing in that town's local directory, a mention in its community forum — these create the association that gets you into the running. It's slower than editing your own pages, but it's the signal that actually convinces the machine.
Is it dishonest to claim towns where I don't have a physical office?
Not at all — as long as you genuinely serve them. A service-area business serving a region without an office in every town is completely legitimate, and documenting that real coverage is just making a truth legible. The line is between documenting real service and fabricating a presence. Claiming towns you actually work in, have customers in, and show up in is honest and smart. Blanketing a hundred towns you've never set foot in with manufactured location pages is the opposite, and answer engines are getting better at catching it. Claim your real territory, reinforce it with real evidence, and stop there.
