The Numbers You Claim About Yourself: How AI Treats "10,000 Customers" and "20 Years in Business"

Scott TischlerFounder & Chairman, AIrecommend.ai · November 23, 2026 · 8 min read
⤓ Download PDFVisit AIrecommend.ai

AI models are drawn to specific numbers — they're concrete, quotable, and easy to drop into an answer. But models also quietly discount numbers that only you assert about yourself. A figure that appears only on your homepage, especially a round and flattering one, gets treated as marketing. The same figure corroborated anywhere else gets treated as a fact. If you want AI to repeat the numbers you claim, the work isn't making them bigger — it's making them specific, dated, and verifiable somewhere other than your own site.

Here's a tension I run into constantly when I look at how AI describes businesses. On one hand, models clearly prefer concrete numbers. Ask an AI to recommend a provider and the ones that come back with specifics — founded in a certain year, a certain number of locations, a measurable track record — tend to sound more authoritative in the answer than the ones described in adjectives. Numbers are quotable in a way that "industry-leading" never is.

On the other hand, models are skeptical of numbers in a very particular way: they've been trained on so much marketing copy that they've effectively learned what puffery looks like. "Over 10,000 satisfied customers" is a phrase that appears on tens of thousands of websites, almost always unverified, almost always rounded to a marketable figure. A model has seen that pattern enough times to treat it as a genre, not as data. So you get this split: AI wants your numbers, but doesn't automatically believe the ones you say about yourself.

Why self-reported numbers get discounted

Think about how a careful human researcher handles a company's self-description. If a business says "we've served more than 10,000 customers," the researcher notes it but doesn't write it down as established fact — it's a claim, from an interested party, with an obvious incentive to inflate. If that same number shows up in a news article, an industry database, a regulatory filing, or is implied by thousands of independent reviews, it graduates from claim to fact.

Models approximate this behavior. They don't have a literal fact-checker running, but the training and the retrieval systems around them push in the same direction: a statement that appears in one place, on the source's own site, carries less weight than a statement multiple independent sources corroborate. This is the corroboration effect, and self-reported numbers are where it bites hardest, because the whole category is pre-flagged as the kind of thing sources exaggerate.

The practical result is that your homepage number lives in a kind of limbo. A model might repeat it. It might soften it ("the company says it has served thousands of customers"). It might drop it entirely in favor of something it can stand behind. Which of those happens depends on context you don't control — so the goal is to move the number out of limbo.

Specific beats round. Dated beats vague.

The single cheapest upgrade is specificity. Compare "over 10,000 happy customers" with "11,400 patients across four clinics since 2009." Both might describe the same business. But the second one has the texture of a real figure — it's precise, it's not suspiciously round, it's anchored to a date and a structure. The first one has the texture of a number someone reverse-engineered to sound good in a headline.

Specificity reads as truth because truth is usually specific. Real records produce numbers like 11,400, not 10,000. When you round up to the clean marketable figure, you're not just losing a little precision — you're adopting the exact shape that signals "marketing claim" to both humans and models. I'd rather state the real, odd, specific number every time, even when a rounder one would look tidier.

Dates do similar work. "20 years in business" is a claim that silently expires and that anyone can assert. "Founded in 2004" is a fact that a model can place on a timeline, cross-reference against your domain registration, your first reviews, your earliest press, and your filings. A dated claim invites corroboration; a vague one resists it. Give the model the version it can check.

Corroboration is the whole game

If specificity is the cheap upgrade, corroboration is the real one. A number becomes something AI will repeat on your behalf the moment it exists somewhere other than your own marketing. That doesn't require anything dishonest — it requires your true numbers to show up in the places independent facts live.

Founding year: make sure it's consistent on your about page, your Google Business Profile, your directory listings, and any press. Customer or unit counts: if they're real, they tend to leave traces — review volume on third-party platforms, case studies, a mention in a local news piece, membership in an association that lists you. Track record claims: a number cited in an interview, a podcast, a conference bio, or a byline is far stronger than the same number sitting alone on your site, because now it's been repeated by something that isn't you.

This is why I push clients toward earned mentions so hard. It's not only about reach. Every independent place your real numbers appear is another vote that turns a claim into a fact in the model's eyes. The homepage states it; the outside sources confirm it; the model repeats the confirmed version. You're not manufacturing proof — you're making sure the proof that already exists is findable.

What to stop doing

The mirror image of all this: unverifiable superlatives are worse than useless. "Industry-leading," "world-class," "trusted by thousands," "the region's premier" — these give a model absolutely nothing to cite. They're not numbers, they're not checkable, and they match the exact register models have learned to skip. That's a big part of why vague copy never shows up in AI answers: there's nothing in it a model can lift and stand behind.

So audit your own claims with one question: could an AI quote this and feel safe doing so? "We've grown 40% a year for three years" — maybe, if it's corroborated. "We're one of the most trusted names in the industry" — never, because there's nothing to quote. Every adjective you can replace with a specific, dated, corroborated number is a trade in your favor.

And resist the temptation to inflate. A model that catches your number contradicting an independent source doesn't just drop that number — it learns to trust your other claims less, the same way a person would. Conflicting facts make AI hedge, and a business that hedges its way into every answer is a business that gets recommended in none of them. Accurate and specific beats impressive and shaky.

A test you can run in ten minutes

Want to see this for yourself? Pick one of your headline numbers and go looking for it the way a model's retrieval system would. Search for the figure alongside your company name. Does it appear anywhere other than your own site — a directory, a review platform, an article, a filing, a profile? If the only hit is your homepage, that number is living in the limbo I described, and you now know why AI might not be repeating it.

Then try the claim itself in a model. Ask ChatGPT, Gemini, or Perplexity to tell you about your company and watch which numbers come back and how they're framed. If your figures return hedged ("the company states…") or don't return at all, that's your corroboration gap showing. The fix is never to shout the number louder on your own site. It's to get the true version of it into one or two independent places — a profile you can claim, a directory you belong in, an interview you can land — and then let the model find the confirmation it was missing. You're not gaming anything. You're closing the distance between what's true and what's checkable.

The honest version of this

Let me be straight about what I can and can't promise. I can't hand you a measurement that proves "we made our numbers specific and citations rose." The tooling to attribute AI recommendations that cleanly doesn't exist yet, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. This is my read of how models weigh self-reported figures — built from a lot of time watching what they repeat and what they quietly refuse to — not a law of physics.

But the direction is one I'd bet on, and the cost of acting on it is almost nothing. You're not inventing new numbers. You're taking the true ones you already have, making them specific, anchoring them to dates, and making sure they're confirmed somewhere other than your own homepage. That's honest, it's cheap, and it happens to be exactly the format AI will actually repeat. When being truthful and being visible point the same way, that's the easiest decision you'll make all quarter.

Key takeaways

  • AI models are drawn to specific numbers because they're concrete and quotable — but they quietly discount numbers that only you assert about yourself.
  • A claim gets stronger the moment something other than your own marketing repeats it: a directory, a news mention, a review platform, a filing.
  • Round, suspiciously-marketable numbers ("over 10,000 happy customers") read as puffery; precise, dated, sourced numbers read as facts.
  • A number AI can verify is one it will repeat on your behalf; a number it can't is one it will either hedge, soften, or drop.
  • The move isn't to inflate your claims — it's to make your true numbers specific, dated, and corroborated somewhere other than your homepage.
  • Vague superlatives ("industry-leading," "trusted by thousands") give a model nothing to cite, which is why they never show up in answers.

Frequently asked questions

Will AI just repeat whatever numbers I put on my website?
Sometimes, but less than you'd hope — and that's by design. Models are trained on enough marketing copy to be wary of self-reported superlatives. A number that appears only on your own site, especially a round and flattering one, is something a model may repeat, soften, or ignore depending on the context. The same number corroborated by an independent source is far more likely to come back verbatim. The lesson: don't rely on your homepage being taken at face value.
Are specific numbers really better than round ones?
In my experience, yes — specificity reads as truth. "Serving 11,400 patients across four clinics since 2009" sounds like it came from a record. "Over 10,000 happy customers" sounds like it came from a marketing meeting. Both might be true, but the first gives a model something precise to quote and a shape that looks like a real figure rather than a target. I'd always rather state the real, specific number than round up to something cleaner.
What if my real numbers aren't impressive?
Then lead with the ones that are true and specific rather than reaching for a bigger, vaguer claim. A model would rather cite "a 12-person firm founded in 2011" than parse "one of the region's leading providers." Small-but-precise beats big-but-unverifiable, because the precise version is citable and the vague version is noise. Honesty here isn't just ethics — it's the format that actually gets repeated.
